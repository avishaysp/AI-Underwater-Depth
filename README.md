# Abstract

Estimating the depth of fish from underwater imagery is crucial for accurately assessing fish biomass and ecological studies. Current methodologies often rely on stereo baited remote underwater video systems (stereo-BRUVs) to determine absolute distances, lengths, and biomass of marine species. In this study, we introduce an AI-based approach utilizing monocular imagery to estimate absolute fish distance from the camera. Our pipeline integrates the WaterNet model for underwater image enhancement and DepthAnythingV2 for generating disparity maps, using a known reference object to translate relative disparities into absolute distances. The experimental dataset comprises manually selected images extracted from an extensive dataset of stereo 4K60 underwater videos, paired with annotated species types and ground truth distances. However, the dataset presented several challenges: misidentification of target fish during pixel annotation, inaccuracies in selecting the correct video frame, ambiguity regarding the specific reference point on the fish used for distance measurement, difficulties distinguishing individual fish in crowded frames, and questionable accuracy of some ground truth distance measurements. Our results, derived from comparing predicted distances against these imperfect ground truths, demonstrate moderate accuracy. Our research also provides foundational insights into monocular depth estimation methods in underwater environments, highlighting critical areas for improvement in data collection, annotation accuracy, and processing techniques.
We tackled two challenges at once: (1) transfer learning from terrestrial images to underwater images (2) extracting absolute distance from relative disparity map.
# Introduction
Accurate estimation of fish biomass is essential for ecological monitoring, resource management, and detecting environmental changes, including the identification of invasive species. Stereo baited remote underwater video systems (stereo-BRUVs) have traditionally served as the primary tool for obtaining precise measurements of fish length, depth, and biomass. These measurements allow researchers to estimate population health, biodiversity, and the ecological impacts of invasive species. However, stereo-BRUV setups can be complex and require specialized software for accurate depth extraction.
Motivated by the potential to simplify and reduce the complexity of current fish biomass sampling methods, our project explores the viability of monocular, AI-driven depth estimation as an alternative to stereo-BRUVs. Specifically, our goal is to assess how effectively monocular imagery combined with modern AI methods can approximate stereo-based depth estimation accuracy.
Given that existing depth estimation solutions underwater predominantly rely on stereo vision, there is limited literature or established monocular methods tailored explicitly for underwater use. Stereo-BRUVs, while effective, involve intricate camera setups and sophisticated software, presenting logistical and computational challenges.
In our proposed approach, we leverage the WaterNet model for underwater image enhancement, hypothesizing that correcting color distortions and improving visibility underwater would enhance the performance of DepthAnythingV2, a model primarily trained on terrestrial imagery. Initial observations indicated improved recognition accuracy in certain cases due to this color correction.
The scope of our research is constrained to assessing the effectiveness of the monocular AI approach using existing video datasets, without exploring new or improved methods of data collection. While acknowledging the limitations of our dataset and approach, we managed to create a moderate depth estimation tool. This study also provides foundational insights and identifies critical considerations for future research aiming to optimize monocular depth estimation in underwater environments.
